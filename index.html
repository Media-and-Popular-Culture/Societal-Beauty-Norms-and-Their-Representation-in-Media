About the Project

Why we built this audit

This project examines how media and popular culture influence the way people think about gender. Movies, television shows, advertisements, news, and social media often shape ideas about how women, men, and gender-diverse people should look, act, and live. Through this audit, we analyze how these messages can become social expectations that affect people's opportunities, treatment, and participation in society.

What this project does

This webpage explores one gender-related issue in media and popular culture using the Four-Lens Gender Audit. We examine how media representations create gender norms, explain the concepts behind the issue, identify how these beliefs are learned through socialization, and analyze how they can lead to gender inequality and discrimination. Finally, we propose practical institutional actions that can help create more fair and inclusive media practices.

Objectives

  • Identify a real gender issue found in media and popular culture.
  • Analyze the issue using Chapters 1 to 4 of Gender and Society.
  • Show how media messages influence gender norms, socialization, and discrimination.
  • Present an integrated pathway that connects all four chapters.
  • Recommend realistic institutional actions based on evidence and credible sources.

Why Gender and Society matters

Gender shapes access to opportunity, safety, and voice. Studying it critically helps us recognize patterns we normally overlook, and gives us a shared vocabulary for asking institutions to do better.

Issue Snapshot

The issue we are auditing

Media and Beauty Standards — Issue Title

Beauty Standards in Media

Skin whitening advertisements remain common across television, social media, and digital platforms. Many of these advertisements suggest that fair skin leads to beauty, confidence, popularity, and success. By repeatedly presenting women with similar physical features, media creates a narrow beauty standard that influences how people view themselves and others. Although these advertisements are intended to promote products, they can also strengthen gender stereotypes and unrealistic expectations about appearance. This issue affects confidence, self-esteem, and equal representation, making it important to examine how media contributes to gender norms and how institutions can encourage more inclusive and respectful portrayals.

Media and Popular Culture Women and Young People National (Philippines)

Core Analysis

The Four-Lens Analysis

Chapter 01

Gender and Society as a Field of StudyThe Media & Popular Culture Lens

How public advertising and film frame women through beauty and domestic roles while reserving authority and leadership imagery for men — narrowing beauty standards and sexualizing women in the process.

Concrete Gendered Pattern: The representation of women in beauty and domestic roles vs. men in active, authority, or leadership roles within commercial ads and public media posts. This sometimes includes narrow beauty standards and sexualization.

The Institution / Social Setting: Media and Popular Culture — specifically public advertising platforms, digital marketing, and film.

Underlying Gender Norm: The societal expectation that a woman's primary value is tied to the shape of her body, being young, and domestic appeal, whereas a man's value is linked to dominance, capability, and leadership.

Core Concern (Power / Participation / Safety)

  • Power & Participation: Women are not much seen in roles of structural authority or leadership in mainstream ads, reinforcing male dominance.
  • Safety & Well-being: This treats mostly women, and objectification of women in public media reduces them to visual subjects, contributing to victim-blaming cultures and distorted body standards.

Media Analysis (Two Public Examples Framework)

Example 1 — Public Skincare/Beauty Product Advertisement
Visual Role: A female model framed in soft lighting, focusing heavily on facial perfection and flawless skin.
Language/Tagline: Words focusing on "flawless," "desirable," or "youthful."
Analysis: Reinforces beauty standards and sexualization by positioning women's primary contribution as visual appeal rather than capability.

Example 2 — Public Corporate/Tech Advertisement
Visual Role: A male actor in a suit positioned central/front, giving directions or operating high-tech machinery while female characters sit in supporting or administrative roles.
Language/Tagline: Phrases emphasizing "power," "control," "leadership," and "command."
Analysis: Reproduces male dominance and leadership framing, systematically making female leadership invisible in technical or authoritative sectors.

Institutional Condition & Policy Anchors

Institutional Change Needed: Media regulatory boards and advertising standards councils must use non-sexist portrayal guidelines, promote diverse body representation, and mandate fair gender participation in media leadership roles.

Reference Anchors:

  • Mateo, J. T. (2026). Gender and Society: Instructional Material.
  • Republic Act No. 9710 (Magna Carta of Women): Section 16 mandates the non-derogatory portrayal of women in media and advertising.
  • Republic Act No. 11313 (Safe Spaces Act): Addresses gender-based sexual harassment in public and digital spaces.
Chapter 02

Sex, Gender, SOGIE & SOGIESCThe Conceptual Foundations Lens

Distinguishing sex from gender, and unpacking SOGIE/SOGIESC, so the issue is grounded in the right vocabulary instead of assumptions about identity.

This distinguishes sex from gender and separates the components of SOGIE and SOGIESC. Sex concerns biological and physiological characteristics; gender concerns socially constructed roles, expectations, behaviors, and power relations. Sexual orientation, gender identity, gender expression, and sex characteristics are related but are not interchangeable. A person's appearance or behavior must never be used to assume identity, attraction, or bodily characteristics.

Gender Terms

  • Male
  • Female
  • Man
  • Woman
  • Boy
  • Girl

Connecting the Issue to Chapter 2 Concepts

The concepts of sex, gender, SOGIE, SOGIESC, and gender diversity are connected because they explain different parts of human identity. Sex describes biological characteristics, while gender explains social and personal experiences related to identity and expression. SOGIE and SOGIESC provide broader frameworks for understanding how people experience attraction, identity, expression, and physical characteristics.

These concepts are important in addressing issues such as gender discrimination, stereotypes, and inequality. Understanding gender diversity encourages respect for individual differences and supports the creation of inclusive communities where everyone can participate without fear of prejudice.

Chapter 03

Gender Role SocializationThe Socialization Lens

How family, school, peers, and media repeatedly teach — and reward or punish — narrow beauty ideals until they feel like common sense.

Gender role socialization is the lifelong process through which people learn what society expects from women, men, and gender-diverse individuals. In the context of societal beauty norms, media and popular culture teach people that certain physical appearances are more attractive and socially acceptable than others.

Agents of Socialization

The main agents of socialization related to beauty norms are:

  • Family
  • Schools
  • Peers
  • Social media
  • Television, movies, and advertisements
  • Influencers and celebrities

These agents repeatedly promote specific beauty ideals, shaping how people view themselves and others.

Gender Script / Message

The common message is that women should be slim, fair-skinned, youthful, and attractive, while men should be tall, muscular, and physically strong. These expectations become social norms that many people feel pressured to follow.

Reinforcement (Reward for Conformity)

People who match these beauty standards often receive:

  • Positive attention and compliments
  • Greater confidence and popularity
  • Better opportunities in media, modeling, or even employment
  • More positive treatment from others

Punishment for Nonconformity

People who do not meet these beauty norms may experience:

  • Body shaming
  • Bullying or teasing
  • Discrimination
  • Lower self-esteem
  • Social exclusion

Affected Group

Societal beauty norms affect everyone, but they particularly impact:

  • Women and girls
  • Men and boys
  • Gender-diverse individuals
  • Teenagers and young adults who are highly exposed to social media

Life-Choice / Participation Effect

Beauty norms can heavily influence people's confidence, education, careers, relationships, and participation in society. Some individuals may avoid social activities, job opportunities, or leadership roles because they feel like they do not meet society's expectations of beauty.

Reference

  • Digennaro, S. & Tescione, A. (2024). Scrolls and self-perception, navigating the link between social networks and body dissatisfaction in preadolescents and adolescents: a systematic review. frontiersin.org
Chapter 04

Gender Inequality, Bias & DiscriminationThe Systemic Lens

How a single repeated beauty ideal turns into unequal treatment — and which laws, agencies, and reforms could close that gap.

Gender Inequality

Gender inequality happens when media and ads keep showing only one version of beauty: fair skin, slim bodies, and muscular physiques. People who don't look like this often get less attention or representation (WHO, n.d.).

Gender Bias

Gender bias is when certain looks are always shown as more attractive or successful. Over time, these repeated images make unrealistic standards seem normal (RA 9710, 2009).

Discrimination

Discrimination based on appearance happens when those who don't fit beauty standards are shown less positively or even negatively. Examples include skin-whitening ads, edited influencer posts, and campaigns that rarely show different body types or skin tones (OHCHR, n.d.).

Type of Discrimination

  • Structural discrimination — the system itself promotes narrow beauty standards.
  • Indirect discrimination — people outside these ideals are disadvantaged even if they are not openly excluded (WHO, n.d.; OHCHR, n.d.).

Institutional Actions (Immediate Protective Response)

  • Disclosure labels on digitally altered or edited images.

Structural / Institutional Actions

  • Diversity in advertising — require campaigns to show different body types, skin tones, and appearances.
  • Media literacy in schools — teach students how to analyze beauty standards critically, so they can recognize bias and stereotypes.

Responsible Institutions

  • Philippine Commission on Women (PCW) — lead agency for RA 9710.
  • Department of Education (DepEd) — for curriculum integration.
  • Ad Standards Council (ASC) — industry enforcer for disclosure and diversity standards.

References

  • Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Human Rights. (n.d.). ohchr.org
  • Republic Act No. 9710 (2009). Magna Carta of Women. lawphil.net
  • World Health Organization. (n.d.). Gender. who.int

Synthesis

The Integrated Pathway

How the four lenses connect into a single continuous chain — from a private habit to a public, institutional response.

Step 01 · Chapter 01

Gender Norm

Cultural norms often promote the idea that women should be slim, fair-skinned, and youthful, while men should be tall, muscular, and physically strong.

Step 02 · Chapter 02

Concepts / Diversity

Beauty standards are social expectations, not biological requirements. People of all sexes, genders, and SOGIESC should be respected regardless of physical appearance.

Step 03 · Chapter 03

Socialization

Cultural influences and advertisements reinforce these beauty ideals, leading many people to believe they must conform to be accepted or valued.

Step 04 · Chapter 04

Inequality & Discrimination

People who do not meet society's beauty standards may experience body shaming, bullying, discrimination, unequal opportunities, and lower self-esteem.

Outcome

Institutional Change

To mitigate appearance-based bias, schools must teach body positivity, media outlets must diversify content, and workplaces must enforce strict, merit-based hiring policies.

Recommendations

Institutional Actions

Three concrete, evidence-based actions institutions can take in response to this audit.

Recommended

Immediate Protective Response

Advertising companies should immediately review advertisements before they are released to ensure that they do not promote unrealistic beauty standards or harmful gender stereotypes. Advertisements that encourage discrimination or imply that one appearance is superior should be revised or removed.

In Progress

Realistic Institutional Changes

Structural Change 1

The Advertising Standards Council of the Philippines (ASC), together with media organizations, should strengthen gender-responsive advertising guidelines that promote diverse representation of different skin tones, body types, ages, and appearances. This can help reduce gender bias and encourage more inclusive media content.

Structural Change 2

Schools, media organizations, and government agencies should strengthen media literacy programs that teach people how to recognize unrealistic beauty standards, gender stereotypes, and misleading content in advertisements and social media. These programs can help audiences become more critical and responsible media consumers.

Proposed

Who Should Implement Them

Advertising Standards Council (ASC)

Develops and enforces advertising standards to promote fair and responsible media content.

Philippine Commission on Women (PCW)

Promotes gender equality and supports policies that prevent gender discrimination.

Department of Education (DepEd)

Integrates media literacy and gender sensitivity into the school curriculum.

Media companies and advertising agencies

Ensure that advertisements represent diverse and inclusive standards of beauty.

Television networks and social media platforms

Monitor and regulate published content to reduce harmful stereotypes and discriminatory portrayals.

In Our Own Words

Reflection

Working on this Gender and Society project helped our group understand that gender issues are not only found in laws or workplaces but also in the media we see every day. While studying beauty standards in advertisements, we learned how repeated images and messages can shape the way people think about beauty, confidence, and success. We realized that media has a strong influence on how society views women, men, and gender-diverse people.

Using the Four-Lens Gender Audit helped us look at the issue from different perspectives instead of making quick conclusions. We learned how gender norms are formed, how they are passed through media and society, and how they can lead to bias, stereotypes, and discrimination. We also understood that institutions such as media companies, schools, government agencies, and advertisers have an important role in promoting fair and respectful representation.

Creating the webpage also improved our teamwork and technical skills. Each member had a specific responsibility, from researching information and analyzing the issue to designing the infographic and developing the website. By working together, reviewing each other's work, and following the grading rubric, we were able to create a project that was organized, informative, and visually appealing.

This project reminded us that small messages in advertisements and social media can have a lasting effect on people's beliefs and behavior. As students, we learned the importance of thinking critically about the media we consume and supporting a society where everyone is respected regardless of their appearance, gender, or identity. We hope this project encourages others to recognize harmful stereotypes and promote more inclusive and positive representation in media.

— Group CZA

Good to Know

Frequently Asked Questions

The Four-Lens Gender Audit is a way of analyzing a gender issue from four connected perspectives. Chapter 1 identifies the gender pattern and social setting. Chapter 2 explains the issue using concepts of sex, gender, and SOGIESC. Chapter 3 examines how gender roles are learned through socialization. Chapter 4 analyzes gender inequality, bias, and discrimination, then proposes institutional actions that can create positive change.

Gender and Society helps students understand how gender norms influence everyday life, education, work, and media. It encourages critical thinking about stereotypes, promotes respect for diversity, and prepares students to support fairness, equality, and inclusion in their communities.

The evidence was gathered from publicly available advertisements, official government documents, course materials, and trusted international organizations. Sources were selected because they are credible, relevant to media and popular culture, and support the analysis with reliable information. Public examples were chosen to protect individual privacy while showing real gender issues.

The findings of this audit can help media organizations, schools, advertisers, and government agencies recognize harmful gender stereotypes and improve policies. The recommendations encourage fair representation, inclusive advertising, media literacy, and stronger gender-responsive practices that promote equality and reduce discrimination.
