About the Project

Why we built this audit

This project examines how media and popular culture influence the way people think about gender. Movies, television shows, advertisements, news, and social media often shape ideas about how women, men, and gender-diverse people should look, act, and live. Through this audit, we analyze how these messages can become social expectations that affect people's opportunities, treatment, and participation in society.

What this project does

This webpage explores one gender-related issue in media and popular culture using the Four-Lens Gender Audit. We examine how media representations create gender norms, explain the concepts behind the issue, identify how these beliefs are learned through socialization, and analyze how they can lead to gender inequality and discrimination. Finally, we propose practical institutional actions that can help create more fair and inclusive media practices.

Objectives

  • Identify a real gender issue found in media and popular culture.
  • Analyze the issue using Chapters 1 to 4 of Gender and Society.
  • Show how media messages influence gender norms, socialization, and discrimination.
  • Present an integrated pathway that connects all four chapters.
  • Recommend realistic institutional actions based on evidence and credible sources.

Why Gender and Society matters

Gender shapes access to opportunity, safety, and voice. Studying it critically helps us recognize patterns we normally overlook, and gives us a shared vocabulary for asking institutions to do better.

Issue Snapshot

The issue we are auditing

Media and Popular Culture: Issue Snapshot

Beauty Standards and Gender Representation

Media and popular culture strongly influence how people see gender and what they believe is acceptable for women, men, and gender-diverse people. In advertisements, movies, social media posts, and other popular content, certain gender roles are often repeated. Women may be judged more by their appearance, while men may be shown as strong, dominant, or natural leaders. Some people are also sexualized, blamed for harmful experiences, or left out of important roles. When these images appear repeatedly, they can make stereotypes seem normal. This matters because media can affect confidence, choices, relationships, and how society treats different genders. Looking closely at these messages helps us identify unfair patterns and promote more balanced representation.

Chapter 02

Sex, Gender, SOGIE, SOGIESC and Gender DiversityThe Conceptual Foundations Lens

Understanding sex, gender, SOGIE, SOGIESC, and gender diversity helps explain the different ways people experience identity, expression, attraction, and sex characteristics.

Sex

  • Biological characteristics: chromosomes, hormones, reproductive organs, anatomy.
  • Classified as male, female, or intersex (natural variations in sex traits).

Gender

  • Social, cultural, and personal identity aspects.
  • Includes how a person identifies, such as man, woman, or non-binary, and expresses gender through behavior, clothing, and appearance.

SOGIE

SOGIE stands for Sexual Orientation, Gender Identity, and Gender Expression. It describes how people experience and express their gender and sexuality. Everyone has a SOGIE, regardless of whether they are LGBTQIA+ or not.

  • Sexual Orientation: Emotional, romantic, or sexual attraction, such as heterosexual, homosexual, bisexual, or asexual.
  • Gender Identity: Internal sense of gender, such as man, woman, both, or neither.
  • Gender Expression: External presentation of gender through clothing, hairstyle, behavior, voice, and mannerisms.

SOGIESC

SOGIESC stands for Sexual Orientation, Gender Identity and Expression, and Sex Characteristics. It recognizes the diverse ways people experience their identity, attraction, bodies, and self-expression.

Adds Sex Characteristics: Physical/biological sex traits such as chromosomes, reproductive organs, hormones, and secondary sex traits, including variations seen in intersex people.

Components of SOGIESC

  • Sex Characteristics: Physical sex traits and natural variations.
  • Sexual Orientation: Romantic, emotional, or sexual attraction.
  • Gender Identity: Internal gender sense.
  • Gender Expression: External gender presentation.

This framework helps understand the complexity and diversity of human identities beyond simple binary categories.

Gender Script / Message

Media promotes ideals about appearance, behavior, and gender roles.

Reinforcement / Reward for Conformity

Praise, acceptance, popularity, and opportunities.

Punishment for Nonconformity

Body shaming, stereotypes, criticism, and discrimination.

Affected Group

Women, men, gender-diverse individuals, and youth.

Life-Choice / Participation Effect

Chapter 04
⚖

Gender Inequality & DiscriminationThe Systemic Lens

Repeated media stereotypes can create unequal representation, unfair treatment, and discrimination based on gender and SOGIE.

Bias

Men are often viewed as better leaders, while women and LGBTQIA+ people may face stereotypes about their roles (Mateo, 2026).

Stereotype

Women are expected to focus on beauty, while men are expected to be strong and successful (Mateo, 2026).

Type of Discrimination

The issue can involve structural and SOGIE-based discrimination when women and LGBTQIA+ people experience repeated stereotypes or exclusion (United Nations, n.d.).

Visible Manifestation

Women and LGBTQIA+ people may be shown in limited or stereotypical roles in media (United Nations, n.d.).

Group Most Affected

Women and LGBTQIA+ people may experience unfair or limited representation (United Nations, n.d.).

Institutional Effect

Repeated stereotypes can affect equal participation and create exclusion in media and society (Republic Act No. 11313, 2019).

Institutional Action

Immediate response: Provide a safe reporting system.

Structural Changes

  • Implement gender-sensitive policies.
  • Provide regular media literacy and gender awareness training.

In Our Own Words

Reflection

Working on this project helped us understand how strongly media and popular culture can shape the way people see gender. We learned that advertisements, movies, social media, and other popular content can repeat ideas about how women, men, and gender-diverse people should look and behave.

The Four-Lens Analysis helped us understand the issue step by step. We saw how gender norms appear in media, how concepts such as sex, gender, SOGIE, and SOGIESC help us understand diversity, and how people learn gender expectations through family, peers, schools, and media. We also learned that repeated stereotypes can lead to unequal treatment and discrimination.

We realized that media representation can affect confidence, identity, choices, and participation in society. Because of this, media creators, schools, organizations, and other institutions have a role in promoting fair and inclusive representation. Media literacy can also help people question stereotypes instead of simply accepting what they see.

This project also helped us improve our teamwork and technical skills. We divided tasks, shared ideas, researched our topic, and worked together to organize the information into a website. Overall, we learned that responsible media representation can help create a more respectful environment where people are valued regardless of their gender, identity, or appearance.

Good to Know

Frequently Asked Questions

The Four-Lens Gender Audit is a way of analyzing a gender issue from four connected perspectives. Chapter 1 identifies the gender pattern and social setting. Chapter 2 explains the issue using concepts of sex, gender, and SOGIESC. Chapter 3 examines how gender roles are learned through socialization. Chapter 4 analyzes gender inequality, bias, and discrimination, then proposes institutional actions that can create positive change.

Gender and Society helps students understand how gender norms influence everyday life, education, work, and media. It encourages critical thinking about stereotypes, promotes respect for diversity, and prepares students to support fairness, equality, and inclusion in their communities.

The evidence was gathered from publicly available advertisements, official government documents, course materials, and trusted international organizations. Sources were selected because they are credible, relevant to media and popular culture, and support the analysis with reliable information. Public examples were chosen to protect individual privacy while showing real gender issues.

The findings of this audit can help media organizations, schools, advertisers, and government agencies recognize harmful gender stereotypes and improve policies. The recommendations encourage fair representation, inclusive advertising, media literacy, and stronger gender-responsive practices that promote equality and reduce discrimination.
